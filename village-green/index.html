Magnesium: why the form on the label matters

Magnesium is an essential mineral involved in hundreds of processes in the body, including muscle and nerve function and energy production. Supplements come in several forms, such as citrate, oxide and glycinate. The form changes how the product is made and how the maker describes its absorption.

Magnesium glycinate pairs magnesium with the amino acid glycine. Makers of glycinate products, including Pathway’s Magnesium Glycinate 400 mg, describe it as a highly absorbable form that does not depend on stomach acid. Magnesium citrate is a common alternative and shows up in drink-mix sticks.

What to check on the label

  • Elemental magnesium per serving, not only the total weight of the compound.
  • How many capsules make one serving. Some products need three capsules for the stated amount.
  • Allergen and filler statements (dairy, soy, gluten, artificial colors).

Ask a professional first if

You take prescription medication, have kidney concerns, or are pregnant or nursing.

Creatine monohydrate: the form most studied

Creatine is made in small amounts by the body and also comes from foods such as red meat and seafood. Creatine monohydrate is the form used in most published research on exercise performance, which is why many brands sell it in plain powder form.

Pathway’s Creatine Monohydrate lists 5 grams of creatine monohydrate per serving, one teaspoon. The brand’s suggested use describes an optional five-day loading phase followed by one or two servings a day.

What to check on the label

  • That the ingredient is creatine monohydrate and that the serving size is stated in grams.
  • Whether the product is flavored or unflavored, and what sweeteners are used.
  • Third-party testing or purity claims, and who makes the raw material.

Ask a professional first if

You have kidney or liver concerns, take regular medication, or are under 18.

Collagen peptides: types and sources

Collagen is the main structural protein in skin, tendons and connective tissue. Supplements use hydrolyzed collagen, which means the protein has been broken into smaller peptides. Labels often list “types” of collagen, which depend on the animal source.

Pathway’s Collagen Peptides Powder is made from grass-fed bovine collagen, types 1 and 3, with 20 grams per serving. Its Multi-Source Collagen blends bovine, fish, chicken bone broth and eggshell membrane sources, so it is not suitable for people avoiding those allergens.

What to check on the label

  • The animal source, especially if you avoid fish, egg or beef.
  • Grams of collagen per scoop, and whether the scoop size matches the serving.
  • Flavoring and sweetener, since unflavored versions have the simplest ingredient list.
Probiotics: strains matter more than the brand name

A probiotic label should name the genus, species and strain of each organism, not only a count. Two products with the same species can contain different strains that have been tested differently. Some newer products use organisms such as Akkermansia muciniphila, which is sold as a single-strain supplement.

Pendulum’s Akkermansia and Metabolic Daily are examples of strain-specific products. The maker’s marketing mentions GLP-1 support. Treat that as the brand’s claim and read the supporting research it cites before you decide.

What to check on the label

  • Full strain names, and the amount in CFU guaranteed through the end of shelf life.
  • Storage instructions. Some products need refrigeration.
  • Whether the product is a standalone strain or a multi-strain blend.

Ask a professional first if

You have a weakened immune system, a serious illness, or take medication for a chronic condition.
